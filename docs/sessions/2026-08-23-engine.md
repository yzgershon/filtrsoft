## 1. Yish audited all 69 listings

Output: `docs/testset/audit-results-2026-08-23.json`, analysis in `docs/CALIBRATION-2026-08-23.md`.

**35 agree · 4 partial · 30 disagree** — and of the corrections, **16 harsher, 10 softer, 6 out-of-scope.**

51% agreement is the wrong number to look at. The disagreements were *systematic*, which meant they were missing rules rather than noise. Nine findings came out of it. The four that changed the architecture:

### The BUY threshold was fundamentally wrong (finding 1)
I had been valuing at **market**. Yish buys at **flip margin**. His note on the $650 i7-6700K build: *"A good deal on this would be like $250"* — against my "fair" of $380–450.

But it isn't a fixed discount either. His own audit disproves that: he **passed** on an Optiplex at 40–65% off, and **bought** an M1 iMac at only 17–37% off. Percentage is an output, not an input.

The real drivers, derived from his 69 calls: **absolute dollars** (every BUY cleared ~$100+, most $250+), **liquidity** (dead categories veto margin), **desirability** (current-gen gaming moves, old business laptops sit), and **condition** (port damage killed his best percentage on the board).

Then he stated it directly: *"under $100 profit im not leaving, under $150 im not driving more than 30min... **but thats just me**. others would drive 20min for $60 profit."*

Those two points resolve to **one parameter — an effective hourly floor around $120–135** — and the "but that's just me" means **verdicts are computed relative to the user, not globally.** Same listing is honestly BUY for one person and PASS for another. No competitor does this.

### My market data was stale (finding 2)
I called an $800 64GB DDR5 kit "4× retail." Yish: *"there is a crazy ram shortage and you can expect to pay for ddr5 32gb ram like $400+."* He also raised both GPUs I'd called overpriced.

Three component calls wrong in the same direction. **Strongest possible argument for the comps archive** — static price knowledge decays and the market moved underneath it.

### Sell-through is a hard veto (finding 3)
He rejects whole categories on liquidity regardless of price: **VR** (*"gets boring after 2 weeks and people know that"*), **pre-2019 Intel Macs**, **datacenter GPUs**. A good price on something that won't sell is not a deal. Now encoded as `liquidity='dead'` on 8 SKUs.

### Cameras cut
He marked all 8 camera listings UNKNOWN — *"i dont know anything about cameras."* Camera gear comps *fine* on eBay; the **risk layer** is what he can't supply. And a category he can't audit is one the engine could be wrong about indefinitely. Cut.

### Other findings
- **Listing liveness is platform-specific.** Craigslist doesn't prune, so old listings are often just dead. Facebook does.
- **Ports/hinges are FUNCTIONAL, never cosmetic** — he opened the Latitude 5520 photos and saw port damage where the seller said "scratches."
- **The Lightning catch** — he spotted a listing offering a Lightning cable with an iPhone 15 (USB-C since 2023). Now a rule in `compatibility.ts`.
- **Negotiation is part of the verdict** — he messaged the iPhone 16 seller and got $800 → $750 mid-audit. Asking price is not fixed; the engine emits a target offer.
- **The $100 floor is NET after sale**, and **channel routing** matters: bigger than a laptop → Facebook only (which retroactively explained two PASSes I couldn't account for).

## 2. His insight that reframed the product

> *"description length can say alot about how much someone knows about their own computer and alot of the time if someone doesnt know alot about what they have then they are more likely to under cut themselves"*

This became `RISK-MODEL.md` section 3.5. **Seller knowledge predicts pricing accuracy but not price direction.** Low knowledge = mispriced, and which way is essentially random:

- low knowledge + underpriced → the classic flip
- low knowledge + overpriced → anchored to what they paid

**That unified two things I had filed separately:** the $800 RAM cluster and the underpriced NZXT build are the *same phenomenon*. Only direction differs.

**Product consequence, and it's an inversion:** a listing with no specs isn't just hard to value, it's a *lead*. The reason it has no specs is the same reason it's cheap. Low-sophistication listings should be **prioritised** for expensive analysis, not skipped — the opposite of how a naive funnel would rank them.

Also corrected **3e**: clustering is not inherently a red flag. The Latitude 5520 and 7420 are almost certainly the same off-lease refurbisher, and both are among the best deals found. Cluster must be weighted by listing quality, same as listing age.

## 3. Sequencing error he caught

I had been treating the eBay API as a hard blocker on all of Phase 1. He pushed back: *"why cant we do the remainder of phase 1 without the ebay keys?"*

**He was right.** eBay is a *data-volume* blocker, not a *build* blocker. Catalog matching, channel/fee math and verdict assembly need no API at all; valuation needs comp *data*, not the *API*. Built all of it with hand-entered provisional comps to prove the path.

He also called out that my time estimates were invented. They were. Dropped that framing.

## 5. Bugs worth remembering

Every one of these was caught by testing against listings whose answers were already known. None would have surfaced otherwise.

1. **CPU regex counted 3 digits, not 4** — never matched `i7-6700K`, so the Gen 4 impossibility silently never fired
2. **Trailing `\b` after model numbers** — can't match `i7-1185G7` because a letter follows the digits
3. **Storage regex required 2+ digits** — reported "1TB" as missing storage
4. **No negation handling** — "zero scratches or dents" read as *disclosing* dents
5. **Category order** — "Gaming Desktop PC / RTX 3050" classified as a GPU, applying component value fields to a whole machine
6. **`damage[0]` on a string** returned `"c"` as evidence
7. **RAM/VRAM read as storage** — "RTX 3050 8GB" became 8GB of storage
8. **Regexing the model's prose** — port check fired on *"no visible damage."* Fix wasn't a better regex, it was **asking for a boolean.**
9. **Sum-of-parts priced a whole PC off its GPU alone** and confidently said PASS. Coverage guard added.

**Killed a feature after testing it:** heuristic spec-screen detection scored 5/10 — but only by predicting "not a screen" every time. Never fired a true positive. Features didn't separate the classes (highest-saturation image was a phone lock screen; highest-detail was a motherboard). Deleted rather than tuned; vision does it trivially.
